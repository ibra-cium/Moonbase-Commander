## What NASA asks for

Space-themed learning content for young students tends to either oversimplify the engineering trade-offs of a real mission, or present them in a way that is too complex to keep a young learner interested. The challenge is to design and build an interactive game or app in which students run a lunar or Martian outpost and balance competing demands such as life support, radiation shielding, power and food production, so they experience first-hand the decisions that make a mission succeed or fail.

The full challenge statement, with NASA datasets, is released on 28 October 2026. We will update this page then.

## How Moonbase Commander answers it

| What the challenge says | What that means for us | How our game does it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Not too simple, not too complex | A young student can play it, and the science stays honest | Few choices, but each one has real consequences. The debrief uses plain words. |
| Tangible and fun | It must feel like a game, not a lesson | Players see the effects on the base: lights go dark, plants wilt, alarms sound. |
| An interactive game or app | Something playable | A browser game that opens from one link on a phone or laptop. |
| Run a lunar or Martian outpost | The student is in charge | The player is the base commander. We start with the Moon; Mars is a later expansion. |
| Balance life support, shielding, power and food | The systems must be linked | Every choice costs something from another system: cargo mass, power, light or crew time. |
| Decisions decide success or failure | Failure comes from choices, not luck | Every disaster is survivable with good planning, and the debrief traces the decisions that led to the result. |
